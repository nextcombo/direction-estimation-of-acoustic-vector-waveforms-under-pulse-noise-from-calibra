# Direction Estimation of Acoustic Vector Waveforms under Pulse Noise: From Calibration and Compensation to Robust Sparse Reconstruction [with MATLAB Code]

Under pulse noise, direction estimation of acoustic vector array: from calibration compensation to robust sparse reconstruction in underwater target detection. Non-orthogonal biases and pulse noise are like two mountains, squeezing traditional algorithms to the point of breathlessness. Today, we discuss from OMMC calibration compensation to reweighted ADMM robust sparse reconstruction, seeing how a complete solution chain unfolds.
1. The starting point of the problem: imperfect sensors
A simple thought experiment: you have deployed several acoustic vector sensors underwater, each capable of simultaneously perceiving sound pressure and two orthogonal directions of particle velocity. Theoretically, the three axes are strictly orthogonal, with uniformly arranged steering vectors—the array signal processing in textbooks is based on this assumption.
But reality is that, due to manufacturing processes, installation tolerances, environmental aging, every link in the process could lead to sensor axes deviating from the ideal orthogonal position. Even a slight deviation of just 10 degrees can cause MUSIC spectrum peaks to drift, split, or even disappear completely.
This is what we call non-orthogonal biases—a seemingly insignificant issue that can break through all algorithmic models based on ideal models.
This bias can be described in a simple mathematical form. A 3×3 bias matrix for a single vector sensor's three axes (sound pressure, x-direction velocity, y-direction velocity) can be represented, its structure surprisingly simple: the first row and column are projections of unit vectors, while the core 2×2 subblock corresponds to the angle matrix between the two velocity axes. When the bias angle is zero, the matrix degenerates into an identity matrix; as the bias angle increases, the coupling between the velocity components also intensifies.
More challenging is that each sensor's bias situation might vary—some may be offset by 8 degrees to the left, others by 12 degrees to the right. The entire array's bias matrix is a block diagonal structure, with each diagonal block corresponding to a local bias of a sensor. Simple modeling is easy to do, but it's not so easy to calibrate one by one.
2. OMMC: leverage auxiliary sources to move calibration forward
The most direct idea is: Can we measure the extent of the deviation first, then compensate for it?
This is the core idea behind manifold matrix compensation.
How to do it? Place an auxiliary sound source in the known direction. Since you know where it is, you can compare the difference between "the actual direction it should point" and "the direction estimated by the algorithm." This difference essentially represents the system error caused by bias.
With this clue, you can search for the optimal deviation angle within a reasonable range of angles. Traverse candidate deviation angles one by one, construct compensation matrices and apply them to the received data, then re-estimate the angle of the auxiliary source using the MUSIC algorithm. Which deviation angle makes the estimation result closest to the true position? That's the optimal calibration parameter.
Construct a compensation matrix with this optimal deviation angle, perform an inverse operation on the received data—essentially "pulling the data back" to the ideal array framework. Then run the MUSIC again, and the spectral peaks will be much more accurate.
From the spectrum plot, the uncalibrated MUSIC spectral peaks are wide and biased, with deviations reaching several degrees; after OMMC calibration, the spectral peaks become sharp and centered, with errors compressed to sub-degree levels. For single target scenarios, this method is simple, effective, and easy to implement in engineering.
But that's not the end of the story. Single target, single auxiliary source is laboratory conditions, but real oceanic environments are far from this.
III. A More Realistic Battlefield: Multi-Target + Impulse Noise + Interference Further pushes the scenario towards reality.
Five targets distributed in different directions. Underwater noise is no longer a gentle Gaussian distribution but an Alpha stable distribution—a statistical model capable of generating extreme values far beyond the probability of a Gaussian distribution, characterized by sharp pulses in the time domain. You can imagine: most of the time, the noise is quiet, but occasionally there is a huge spike without warning that drowns out the signal.
The smaller the characteristic index of the Alpha stable distribution, the stronger the impulsiveness. When the index is 1.2, it has already entered the strong pulse category—traditional second-order statistical measures methods struggle under these conditions.
Meanwhile, there are interference sources in the environment—some are coherent interferences (such as multipath reflections from the seabed or surface), and some are independent non-coherent interferences (such as radiation noise from another ship far away). The interference energy may be stronger than the target signal, further compressing the detection space.
Under this scenario, traditional MUSIC faces three challenges:
The first challenge is non-orthogonality. The array model mismatches, the steering vector deviates from the true value, and the spectral peak position shifts.
The second challenge is alpha stable pulse noise. The estimation of the covariance matrix is severely contaminated by extreme values, the noise subspace overlaps with the signal subspace, and the spectral peak is buried by noise spikes.
The third challenge is multipath and interference. Coherent interference generates additional correlated sources, and the number of spectral peaks may exceed the target number; non-coherent interference raises the noise floor, suppressing weak targets.
The countermeasure strategy for MUSIC-OMMC is as follows: introduce diagonal loading in the covariance matrix estimation stage—artificially adding a small amount to the diagonal of the matrix, equivalent to injecting weak isotropic noise, stabilizing the numerical process of matrix inversion and feature decomposition. The loading amount is taken as one percent of the trace of the covariance matrix, empirically, it is a good compromise between stability and spectral peak resolution.
However, this only alleviates the problem, not eliminating it entirely.
Alternatively, think outside the box: the core of the subspace method from sparse reconstruction lies in the covariance matrix, which is estimated through finite-aperture data. Under pulse noise, this estimation itself has systematic biases—diagnosing the lesion but not addressing the root cause.
Is there a way to bypass the covariance matrix?
Yes. This is the rationale for entering the field of sparse reconstruction.
The basic logic is quite simple. We discretize the entire azimuth angle space—for example, from 0 degrees to 360 degrees—into a dense grid. On this grid, most positions are devoid of signals, with only a few angles corresponding to real targets. This inherently constitutes a sparse representation problem: using an overcomplete directed matrix to represent the received data, where the positions of non-zero elements correspond to target angles and the number of non-zero elements corresponds to the number of targets.
From a mathematical perspective, we need to find a set of coefficient vectors in a high-dimensional space that are as sparse as possible and can explain the observed data. This is a classic sparse optimization problem.
However, sparse reconstruction is equally fragile against pulse noise. The reason lies in the fidelity term used in standard sparse optimization—the L2 norm—which naturally assumes that the residual follows a Gaussian distribution. When extreme values of Alpha stability appear in the residuals, the L2 norm will focus all attention on those outliers, leading to offsets in the entire solution.
This is the fundamental reason why we need a new algorithm.
V. Reweighted ANM-ADMM: A Three-Edged Sword
Our core method is called Robust Reweighted Non-Grid Atomic Norm Minimization-Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers. Its name is long, but essentially it does three things—each corresponding to a challenging aspect.
5.1 The first blade: Nonlinear prefiltering—pre-screening the data before entering the optimization framework with a "threshold." It performs a nonlinear compression on the received signal element by element: each sample value is divided by its own amplitude plus a constant. The effect is that normal amplitude signals remain almost unchanged, while pulse spikes with abnormally large amplitudes are compressed proportionally.
This is not optimal signal processing, but it serves as a very practical "protective shield"—ensuring that subsequent iterations do not diverge due to individual extreme values.
5.2 The second blade: Reweighted l1 norm—approaching the ideal goal of l0 sparse optimization—directly penalizing the number of non-zero elements. However, l0 optimization is NP-hard, and in practice, the l1 norm is often used as a convex relaxation.
The shortcoming of the norm is that it penalizes both large and small coefficients equally, leading to over-penalization of small coefficients and under-penalization of large coefficients—reflected in the spectrum as higher sidelobes and suppressed weak targets.
The idea behind reweighting is: if an angle shows significant energy during the previous iteration, it's likely a true target; next time, assign a smaller penalty weight to it; conversely, if an angle's energy remains very low, it's probably noise or interference; assign a larger penalty—pushing it further towards zero.
The weight is taken as the reciprocal of the energy estimate. In this way, after more than ten iterations, the weights for true target locations automatically decrease, retaining their distinctiveness; the weights for noise and interference locations automatically increase, being cleaned up.
Third blade: Spatial smoothing regularization alone isn't enough. The presence of non-orthogonal deviations means there's artificial coupling between adjacent angles—deviation angles "leak" energy from one direction to neighboring ones. Without constraints, these leaks can produce spurious peaks.
Our approach is to add a spatial smoothing term to the optimization objective. Essentially, it penalizes drastic changes between adjacent coefficients. For true targets, the change between adjacent coefficients is natural; but for the leakage caused by deviations, there usually appears unwarranted fluctuations between adjacent coefficients—the smoothing term precisely suppresses such situations.
ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers) and Adaptive Heat-Sink ADMM are powerful tools for solving the above constrained optimization problem. It decomposes the original problem into three subproblems: one for least squares update of the reconstruction signal, one for reweighted soft threshold shrinkage of sparse coefficients, and one for simple addition of the dual variable. With three steps of iteration, each step has a closed-form solution, making the calculation highly efficient.
The adaptive aspect lies in dynamically adjusting the regularization parameter. At the beginning of iterations, the regularization parameter is large, prioritizing suppression of noise and interference while retaining only the strongest main peak; as iterations progress, the regularization parameter gradually decreases—like a heat-sink, giving weaker targets a chance to "emerge." The lower bound ensures that the algorithm does not overfit to noise.
Sixth, Comparison: The performance of two generations of methods in the same scenario allows us to compare the algorithms in a typical multi-objective scenario. The scene setup is as follows: five targets are distributed at different orientations, with an 10-degree non-orthogonal deviation in the acoustic vector array, and noise is characterized by Alpha stable pulse distribution (feature index 1.2), while both coherent and non-coherent interferences are applied.
The performance of MUSIC-OMMC deserves respect. Diagonal loading keeps it stable in pulse noise and maintains spectral peak discernibility. However, upon closer observation, it is found that the noise baseline of the spectrum is overall higher, and the peaks of weak targets are not prominent enough, and the difference in interference direction and true target spectral peak height is not obvious enough.
The performance of R-ANM-ADMM has made a qualitative leap. Firstly, the spectrum is very clean—background noise is significantly reduced, and spectral peaks are sharp and the base is flat. Secondly, the peak heights of the five targets are close together, without the problem of weak targets being masked. Thirdly, although there is residual interference, its amplitude is far lower than that of target peaks, and precise target localization can be achieved through a simple Top-K filtering. Most importantly, the algorithm's spatial resolution is significantly better, and closely-angled two targets are clearly separated.
Where is the fundamental difference? MUSIC follows a subspace route of "estimating covariance matrix → feature decomposition → spectral peak search," where the quality of the covariance matrix determines the performance limit. R-ANM-ADMM follows an optimization route of "sparse prior + nonlinear filtering + iterative refinement," where each step is fighting against noise—prefiltering to suppress pulses, reweighting to preserve truth from noise, smoothing term to suppress leakage, and annealing to mine weak signals. It does not coexist with noise but gradually and strategically gets rid of it in iterations.
Seventh, Summary and Prospects: From OMMC calibration to R-ANM-ADMM, this technical route reflects a more general rule in the field of signal processing: when model assumptions conflict with real conditions, rather than patching up old frameworks, it is better to reexamine the structure of the problem and seek a more fundamental solution.
The OMMC's problem lies in its assumption that noise characteristics are good, biases can be precisely estimated, and interferences can be controlled—all of which are fragile in real underwater environments. R-ANM-ADMM does not make these assumptions but instead integrates anti-noise, debiasing, and interference suppression into the optimization process itself.
Of course, R-ANM-ADMM also has its own limitations. The trade-off between convergence speed and iteration count, the computational cost of multi-parameter joint tuning, and performance degradation under extremely low signal-to-interference ratio—these issues merit further study. However, from the current comparative results, its robustness advantage in pulsed noise environments is already clear and significant.
For practitioners in engineering, a practical suggestion is: In scenarios with relatively mild noise conditions and well-calibrated sensors, MUSIC-OMMC remains an efficient and reliable choice; once entering complex environments with pulsed noise, strong interferences, or rich multipaths, the robustness advantage of R-ANM-ADMM will become evident. Both methods have their respective domains, and pursuing "absolute best" in the context of the scenario is more important than seeking absolute superiority.
Finally, if you encounter similar problems in practice, evaluate it from three dimensions: Is the noise pulse strong? Is the sensor bias large? Are the interferences coherent or non-coherent? The answers to these three questions will essentially help you select the appropriate algorithmic path.
The technical methods involved in this paper have been validated in multi-objective direction estimation simulations, and related numerical experiments cover multiple non-ideal factors such as Alpha stable distribution pulse noise, non-orthogonal vector sensor bias, coherent/non-coherent interferences, etc.
